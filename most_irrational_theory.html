Every observation is a projection. This is not a philosophical caveat — it is the central fact of measurement. A spectrograph resolves velocities along the line of sight and is blind to transverse motion. A gravitational lens integrates mass along its entire depth and returns a single number. The cosmic microwave background records the universe at one epoch, seen through the geometry of every epoch between then and now. There is no view from nowhere. Every instrument sits at a position, points in a direction, and returns a shadow.

Modern cosmology has accumulated shadows that refuse to agree with each other. The Hubble constant measured from the early universe disagrees with the same constant measured locally — persistently, and with growing statistical confidence. The matter density inferred from the CMB disagrees with matter density measured by gravitational lensing. Galaxies rotate as if they contain far more mass than we can see. The standard response to each disagreement has been to add a new ingredient — dark matter, dark energy, new early-universe physics — to make all the shadows consistent with each other.

This article proposes a different response. The shadows disagree because they are projections of a curved geometry, taken from different positions on that geometry, and compared as if the space between those positions were flat. The anomalies are not gaps in our inventory of the universe's contents. They are the shape of the space between our measurement positions — visible only in the disagreement itself.

One Number, Two Predictions

"Two exact measurements, taken from two different positions on a curved manifold, will always disagree. The disagreement is not error. It is the geometry of the space between them."

The Hubble tension is a disagreement between two measurements of the universe's expansion rate at different cosmic epochs. The early measurement uses the CMB — light from 380,000 years after the Big Bang. The local measurement uses Cepheid variable stars. They return approximately 67 and 73 kilometers per second per megaparsec respectively. The ratio between them is 73/67 ≈ 1.09.

The proposal is that this ratio is a geometric parameter — our current coordinate on a spiral manifold, measured in radians. Call it θ. From θ alone, without further assumption, one non-trivial prediction follows:

From Hubble ratio to matter density θ = Hlocal / HCMB ≈ 1.09 rad ↓ Ωm = 1 / tan²(θ) = 0.2721 Measured independently by weak lensing: Ωm = 0.272  ·  agreement to four decimal places

The matter density of the universe — measured by gravitational lensing, using entirely different physics and entirely different observations — matches the value derived from the Hubble ratio to four decimal places. Two independent measurements. One geometric parameter. The Hubble tension and the matter density disagreement between CMB and lensing are not two separate problems. They are two projections of the same underlying geometric quantity, seen from the same angle.

The Orthogonal Component

The electromagnetic field is a single object. A stationary observer sees only its electric component — the force between charges at rest. A moving observer sees both electric and magnetic components, divided in a ratio that depends on their velocity. Neither observer is wrong. Both are projecting the same field tensor onto their own measurement axes. The magnetic field is not a separate phenomenon hiding inside the electric field. It is the electric field, experienced from a moving perspective.

Galaxy rotation curves measure tangential velocities — motion perpendicular to the line of sight. Stellar masses are inferred from luminosity — light arriving along the line of sight. These are approximately orthogonal projections of the same gravitational field. The "missing mass" is the component of the field's curvature that projects onto neither measurement axis — real in the geometry, invisible to both instruments.

No new substance is required. The galaxy rotation anomaly is the same phenomenon as the Hubble tension: a curved geometry producing different shadows when measured from different angles, with the difference attributed to missing content rather than unaccounted curvature. A century of searching for dark matter particles has returned nothing, because the search is for an object where there is only a projection.

The spiral is not a single global parameter. It is a field — varying across the manifold. Different positions sample different local values. Planets express those local values as axial tilts, the way iron filings express a magnetic field.

We exist at this tilt, with this Moon, in this configuration, not because we were fortunate. We exist here because this is the configuration that generates observers. The survivorship is not luck — it is geometry expressing its own selection criterion.

Uranus is not exceptional. It is the same geometry as Earth, read from a position where the field has crossed its own critical angle.

The CMB as a Fossil

The cosmic microwave background is a snapshot of the universe at recombination — the moment, 380,000 years after the Big Bang, when hydrogen formed and light decoupled from matter. At that epoch, the spiral's geometric coordinate was near its starting value. The geometry was very nearly flat.

ΛCDM fits the CMB's acoustic peaks assuming a flat geometry and extrapolates that flatness forward to the present. It fits very well — because the early universe was indeed nearly flat. The error is the extrapolation. The parameters ΛCDM extracts from the CMB are exact for the epoch of recombination. Applied to the present, they are the geometry of then, projected onto the conditions of now. The Hubble tension and matter density disagreement are precisely these projections, misread as inconsistencies rather than recognized as the accumulated geometric change between two positions on the spiral.

The James Webb Space Telescope has found galaxies at the highest observed redshifts that are too massive, too structured, and too chemically mature for the ages ΛCDM assigns them. In the spiral framework this is expected. Redshift encodes geometric distance along the manifold between emission and observation — not elapsed time in an expanding flat space. Those galaxies had more time. The redshift is a projection of position, not a clock reading.

We mistook their disagreement for error rather than recognizing it as the message.
